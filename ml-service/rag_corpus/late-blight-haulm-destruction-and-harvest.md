## Summary
Once late blight is in the field, your crop of tubers is what you are protecting. The pathogen needs living green leaves and stems to make spores. Kill the foliage, wait, and only then lift. Harvesting too soon, or lifting wet, is how a saved crop is lost in storage.

## Body

### Deal with the first patch the day you find it
A single spot is worth stopping. AHDB advises: "Destroy or remove any hot spot found in the field." Pull out the affected plants, tubers and all. Then go past the visible edge. AHDB says to treat "a few metres beyond the outside edge of the blighted area," because those plants took the most spores and may already be infected without showing it.

Carry what you pull out of the field in a bag. Do not drop it on the bund. Do not chop or beat blighted foliage where it stands. AHDB is explicit: "When destroying infected foliage, do not flail, because this could increase the spread of spores."

### Why killing the haulm works
AHDB states the principle: "The blight pathogen requires green plant tissue in order to develop and produce spores." UC IPM adds the other half: "Phytophthora infestans does not survive very long in dead foliage." Dead haulm makes no spores.

TNAU recommends destroying the foliage a few days before harvest. If the crop has already made the tuber size you need and blight is spreading, cutting the haulm early is the better trade.

### Kill the stems, not just the leaves
Leaves dry first. Stems hold on. AHDB warns that "stem tissue will be slower to desiccate than leaves, therefore, stems remain a source of inoculum for tuber infection for longer." Check for regrowth after you cut. AHDB notes that "blighted regrowth greatly increases the risk of tuber infection." Cut it again.

### Then wait before you lift
This step is the one most often skipped. AHDB: "The generally recommended safe harvest interval to minimise the risk of tuber infection during harvest is a minimum of 14 days after the haulm is completely dead." UC IPM says the same: make sure "vines are completely dead for 2 to 3 weeks before harvest."

Waiting does two things. Spores in the soil lose viability. And already-infected tubers start to rot, so you can see them and leave them out.

### Lift dry, handle gently
Do not lift a blighted crop in wet weather if you can wait for a dry spell. AHDB: "Harvesting under dry conditions will reduce the spread of blight from any infected tubers to healthy ones." If tubers get wet, dry them at once.

Bruises and cuts are entry points. AHDB notes spores "do not require wounds to infect tubers, they can infect through lenticels and eyes," but damage adds openings. TNAU agrees: "Tuber contamination is minimized if injuries are avoided at harvest time."

Sort as you go. Keep rotted or brown-patched tubers out of the store. Wash tools and machinery after handling a blighted lot.

### Store carefully, and check
The bigger danger in store is not the blight itself. It is the soft rot bacteria that follow it. Keep the store cool and dry, with air moving. Open the pile and check for warm, wet spots.

### Clear away the cull heap and the volunteers
This job is the most neglected, and it decides next season. Waste heaps sprout, the sprouts get blight, and spores blow into the new crop. CPRI scientists recommend "covering of dumps with black plastic sheet throughout the season." AHDB adds detail: keep the heap low, seal the sheet edges so spores cannot escape, and site it far from any potato field.

Volunteer plants from tubers you missed do the same. Lift thoroughly. Manitoba Agriculture advises to "promptly remove or destroy volunteer potatoes found in other crops grown in rotations or elsewhere." A dense following crop helps smother them.
