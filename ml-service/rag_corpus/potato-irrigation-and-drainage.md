## Summary
Water is the one input a potato farmer controls every week, and it decides both disease and tuber quality. Too little water at tuber set brings scab and cracked, misshapen tubers. Too much water starves the roots of air, feeds rots, and keeps the leaves wet long enough for late blight. Steady moisture beats a swing from dry to flooded.

## Body

### Steady is the goal
AHDB's guide opens with the principle: "Maintaining consistent soil moisture throughout the growing season is a key factor in producing high-yielding, quality potato crops."

### Early season: the window that decides scab
Common scab is controlled by water, not by a spray. AHDB states that "The critical period for control is between one and three weeks after TI," where TI is tuber initiation, the point at which "stolon ends swelling to twice the width of the stolon." During that window, "Keep the top 25cm of soil moist" and "Always check with a spade to ensure water is wetting the ridge or bed around all tubers."

Stopping too early undoes the work: "Ceasing irrigation two weeks after TI allows pathogens to multiply rapidly and can increase scab infection unless rain falls."

### Too much water is its own disease
AHDB says over-watering "Increases the incidence of tuber cracking and rotting diseases", "Reduces nitrogen uptake", and slows growth "through lack of oxygen supply for root respiration." UC IPM adds that "Excess or poorly timed irrigation may reduce yields and quality, cause several disease problems in the field or in storage," and that late in the season "Excess irrigation at this time may reduce oxygen levels in the soil and cause tuber rot."

Waterlogging shows up at harvest. The UNECE seed potato guide describes rot developing "in tubers from waterlogged soils", where cut tubers "exude water with a sour milk or vinegary smell." Its advice is short: "Ensure adequate soil drainage." TNAU records bacterial soft rot developing best at 21 to 29 degrees C with about 94% relative humidity, which is what a warm waterlogged field provides.

### Irrigate so the leaves dry before night
This is the direct link to late blight. UC IPM warns that "Watering during late afternoon or early evening may allow foliage to stay wet all night, providing a favorable environment for late blight," and advises "do not run solid set sprinklers longer than 6 hours, regardless of time of day."

### Feast and famine cracks the tuber
Physiological cracks are "caused by rapid tuber expansion often triggered by an increase in soil moisture following a dry period." Misshapen and glassy tubers come from "uneven growth conditions, especially a warm period followed by rainfall."

### Why the last irrigation matters
Late in the season the aim is to keep the tubers full without leaving the soil wet. AHDB is explicit: "Crops allowed to accumulate high SMDs prior to defoliation can cause tubers to become dehydrated and increase the risk of bruising." But "If soils are too wet, rotting diseases, lenticel eruption and reduced dry matter accumulation can result." Its advice is to monitor soil moisture in the run-up, "rather than irrigating immediately prior to desiccation and/or lifting." UC IPM puts a number on it: "Final irrigations in most growing areas should be timed to allow soil moisture to drop to about 60% of field capacity at the time of vinekill."

So do not give a heavy irrigation just before you dig. Do not let the field go bone dry either.

### Drainage and beds
Plant on ridges and furrows so water leaves the field. Avoid over-working the soil: AHDB warns that "Excessively fine seedbeds can impede drainage and lead to increased risk of waterlogging." Lift tubers from wet patches separately.

### Local numbers
TNAU's recommendation for Tamil Nadu is to "Irrigate the crop 10 days after planting", then "once in a week." Other soils and seasons need other intervals. Ask your Krishi Vigyan Kendra or the Kisan Call Centre for the schedule used in your district.
