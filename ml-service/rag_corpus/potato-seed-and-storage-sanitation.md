## Summary
Potato is grown from a tuber, not from a true seed. So last year's disease travels into this year's field inside the seed you plant. Most of what you can do about potato disease happens before planting and in the store, not with a sprayer.

## Body

### Why seed is the main road for disease
Every disease in the mother plant can pass into the daughter tubers. NAAS notes that vegetative propagation leaves the seed vulnerable to pathogens right through the multiplication chain. Virus loads build up generation after generation. That is seed degeneration, and it is why yields fall when farmers replant their own seed year after year.

Diseases carried in tubers include late blight, black scurf, common scab, dry rot, soft rot, bacterial wilt and the potato viruses. Some travel invisibly: a bacterial-wilt tuber can look perfectly sound. NAAS also warns that India has no mechanism to monitor seed potato quality, and that degenerated produce is often sold as seed to small and marginal farmers.

### Checks when you buy seed
- Buy certified seed where you can, and ask its class: breeder, foundation or certified.
- Ask which state and season it came from. Hill-grown, cool-season seed degenerates more slowly.
- Reject lots with black lumps, corky patches, wrinkled sunken lesions or any rot.
- Cut a few tubers across from each lot. Browning or a water-soaked look in the vascular ring is a bad sign.

### Cutting and handling
- Do not cut or split seed tubers if you can avoid it. NHB says splitting spreads bacterial wilt even to healthy tubers.
- If you must cut, use a clean knife and clean it often. One infected tuber contaminates every tuber cut after it.
- Let cut surfaces heal before planting. NHB advises drying cut pieces for 24 to 48 hours.
- Never throw or tip tubers. Bruises are the entry doors for dry rot and soft rot.
- Keep lots, varieties and generations separate, and label them.

### Curing before storage
Freshly lifted tubers have soft skin that must harden. The NHB cold-storage standard says potatoes should be cured at 15 to 20 degrees C and 90 to 95 percent humidity for five to ten days before storage, so surface wounds heal, water loss stops and decay is prevented. Harvest when it is neither below 8 nor above 20 degrees C.

### Storage temperature
On ICAR-CPRI advice, the NHB standard takes 3 degrees C plus or minus 1 as the critical storage temperature for seed potato, at 90 to 95 percent humidity. Table potato is given as 4 degrees C. CPRI advises cooling seed to 10 degrees C within 24 hours of arrival, then down to holding temperature over 8 to 10 days.

### If you have no cold store
Most smallholders do not. Then ventilation and light matter more than temperature.
- Store in a cool, dry, shaded place with air moving through.
- Keep tubers in thin layers, not deep heaps. A deep heap builds heat and carbon dioxide in the middle, and that is where sprouting and rot begin. Gaps in the walls, and openings at both bottom and top, let that air escape.
- Seed kept in the dark grows one long, thin, white sprout that breaks easily and establishes badly. Indirect daylight with good airflow gives several short, strong, coloured sprouts instead. CIP's diffused-light-store guidance, from smallholder work in East Africa, reports 30 to 50 percent less rotting and weight loss than dark storage.
- Keep direct sun off the tubers, and keep them dry. Water encourages rot.
- Cover openings with fine net. Aphids feed on sprouts and spread virus; tuber moth enters the same way.
- Sort before storing. Remove every rotten, diseased, bruised or cut tuber. Storage never improves seed quality; it only preserves what you put in.
- Hand-sort every few weeks and remove anything soft, wrinkled or smelling.
- Clean the store out before next season's crop goes in.
