## Summary

Once a potato plant has a virus, it has it for life, and every tuber it makes
carries the virus forward. Nothing you spray will cure that plant. Everything that
works is prevention: clean seed, keeping the vector down, pulling out sick plants,
and cutting the crop before aphids build up.

## Body

### The plant stays infected

ICAR-CPRI states it directly: "Once the plant is infected by virus, it will be
transmitted to generation after generation since potato is being vegetatively
propagated material." Potato is grown from tubers, not true seed. A sick plant makes
sick tubers. Plant those tubers and you plant the virus.

ICAR-CPRI adds: "In most practical cases, viruses can only be eliminated by
eradication of infected plants in seed production programme." Eradication means
removing the plant, not treating it.

### No chemical cures it

ICAR-CPRI states that "Diseases caused by viruses cannot be easily controlled by
chemicals alone." Insecticide can reduce the aphids and whitefly that carry virus
between plants, which protects plants still healthy. It does not repair a plant
already infected.

**A fungicide does nothing at all here.** Fungicides act on fungi. A virus is not a
fungus. Mancozeb, a copper spray or a late blight product will not slow a virus by
any amount. This mistake is common and costly, because mottled and curled leaves can
look fungal to the eye. Money spent on fungicide for a viral crop is lost, and the
virus keeps spreading while you spray.

If you are unsure whether the problem is fungal, viral or a nutrient shortage, find
out before buying anything.

### What actually works

ICAR-CPRI advises managing potato viruses "on co-operative basis and in an
integrated approach" — neighbours must act together, because the vectors fly across
field boundaries.

**Plant clean seed.** ICAR-CPRI's first listed measure is to "Essentially plant the
disease-free seed stocks from approved or reliable sources." This is the biggest
decision. Most virus arrives in the seed tuber, not on the wind.

**Rogue the sick plants.** Pull out and destroy plants showing symptoms. ICAR-CPRI
advises roguing "should be done as soon as the symptoms are visible in order to
remove the source of infection and to prevent the spread of diseases." A rogued
plant cannot infect its neighbours.

**Watch the vector count.** ICAR-CPRI ties action to a threshold: rogue infected
plants and dehaulm "as soon as the vector populations exceeds critical limit of 20
aphids/ 100 compound leaves." Yellow traps of 15 by 30 cm just above canopy height,
60 per hectare, help monitor and trap.

**Clear the reservoirs.** Manage weeds regularly. Remove nearby hosts such as okra,
brinjal and datura. Take out volunteer potato plants — they grow from last season's
infected tubers and carry virus over.

**Cut the haulm before aphids peak.** For a seed crop this ends exposure. Haulm can
be cut manually. Cover exposed tubers with soil at once and remove regrowth.

**Disinfect tools.** PVX, PVS and the spindle tuber viroid need no insect.
ICAR-CPRI warns they "are readily spread in stores and fields through contaminated
hand, clothes of workers, farm machinery/tools etc." Its advice, quoted from the
2016 Model Training Course, is to "disinfect all field equipment's by dipping in or
washing them either with 3% trisodium phosphate or calcium hypochloride (1%)
solution."

*Those two figures are equipment-cleaning strengths, not a spray rate for the crop.
Nothing here goes on the plants. They are quoted as ICAR-CPRI wrote them and are not
this platform's recommendation.*

### On insecticide rates

ICAR-CPRI's course also names specific insecticide seed treatments, vector sprays
and haulm-killing rates. This document deliberately repeats none of them. Any
product and rate must first be checked against the CIBRC register of what is
permitted on potato in India.
