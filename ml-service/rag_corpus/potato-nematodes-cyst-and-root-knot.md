## Summary

Nematodes are worms too small to see. They live in the soil and feed inside
potato roots. You cannot diagnose them from a photo of the leaves, because the
leaf symptoms look like drought or a nutrient shortage. Diagnosis needs a soil
sample.

## Body

### Why a photo will not do

Plant parasitic nematodes are under a millimetre long. ICAR-CPRI notes that
infestations often go unrecognised because the foliage symptoms are mistaken for
nutrient deficiency. An ICAR-CPRI review puts it directly: cyst nematode symptoms
are not specific and resemble other biotic or abiotic stresses.

So the honest answer to a photo of stunted, yellowing plants is: it could be
nematodes, and it could be several other things. Get the soil tested. A test
counts eggs or juveniles in a measured weight of soil, and it can be done before
you plant, while you can still change your plan.

### How to take the sample, and what the number means

Dig soil from the root zone, at several spots across the patch of poor plants and
at its edge. Include root pieces — roots are where the evidence is. Send about
200 to 300 g to a state agriculture department or KVK laboratory. Ask for a
nematode count, not a fertility test.

ICAR-CPRI gives an economic threshold for cyst nematode of around 20 eggs per
gram of soil, and notes it varies with conditions and variety tolerance. For
root-knot it refers to 10 to 20 larvae per 100 g of soil.

Look at the roots yourself while sampling. Cyst nematode shows as pinhead-sized
white or yellow females stuck to the roots. Root-knot shows as swellings, called
galls.

### Potato cyst nematode (Globodera)

Two species: *Globodera rostochiensis*, the golden cyst nematode, and *Globodera
pallida*, the white cyst nematode. The female dies and her body hardens into a
brown cyst holding several hundred eggs. Cysts survive in soil for 20 to 30 years
even with no potato grown.

Field symptoms start as a small patch of stunted plants with pale yellow leaves.
The patch grows year after year if potato is grown continuously.

**The Nilgiris quarantine.** The nematode was first found in India in 1961, at
Ootacamund in the Nilgiris, and later in the Kodaikanal hills. In 1971 the Tamil
Nadu Government imposed domestic quarantine, with strict checking of seed potato
leaving infested fields.

In practice: do not move seed potato, soil, gunny bags, implements or even soil on
your footwear out of an infested area. It also spreads on farm workers, on
livestock and in irrigation water. It is mainly confined to the Nilgiris and
Kodaikanal hills, with low-intensity finds in Karnataka and Kerala.

**Rotation with non-hosts.** Cyst nematode only feeds on the Solanaceae —
potato, tomato and brinjal. That is its weakness. ICAR-CPRI reports a three to
four year rotation with radish, cabbage, cauliflower, turnip, garlic, carrot, or
a green manure such as lupin, cuts the cyst population by more than 50 per cent.
Growing potato in summer and the rotation crop in autumn and spring worked in the
Nilgiris. Intercropping with French bean also reduced cyst numbers.

**Resistant varieties.** Breeding at ICAR-Central Potato Research Station,
Muthorai released Kufri Swarna (1985) and Kufri Neelima (2010), high-yielding
varieties with combined resistance to the local cyst nematode pathotypes and to
late blight.

**Other measures.** Soil solarisation, trap crops, neem cake and biocontrol
agents such as *Pseudomonas fluorescens* and *Paecilomyces lilacinus*. Once
established, cyst nematode is very hard to eradicate. The goal is to hold it low
enough to grow a profitable crop.

### Root-knot nematode (Meloidogyne)

Four species occur on potato in India: *M. incognita*, *M. javanica*, *M. hapla*
and *M. arenaria*. *M. incognita* dominates in hills and plains. Unlike cyst
nematode, root-knot has a very wide host range — over 2000 plants — so rotation
is harder and weeds keep it alive.

Above ground: stunting and yellowing. On the roots: galls. On the tubers: warty
pimple-like blemishes that make them unsaleable, and brown spots in the flesh
when cut. Root-knot infection also makes potato more likely to get early blight
and brown rot, so a nematode problem can surface as a disease problem.

**Management.** Plant nematode-free tubers, never seed from an infested area.
Deep plough and sun-dry the soil in summer. Keep the field weed-free. Rotate with
non-hosts such as maize or wheat. Grow marigold (*Tagetes patula*) as a trap crop
in alternate rows. Adjust planting dates — first week of January, or the second
to third week of October — because lower temperatures reduce infection.

### A note on chemicals

ICAR-CPRI names nematicides and soil fumigants with rates. None are repeated
here. Nematicides are among the most hazardous farm chemicals, and any rate must
be checked against the CIBRC register first.
