## Summary
Bacterial wilt, also called brown rot, is caused by the bacterium *Ralstonia solanacearum*. It is a soil and seed problem, not a spray problem. No spray reaches it. You can test for it yourself with a cut stem and a glass of clean water: if milky threads stream down from the cut end, you very likely have it.

## Body

### What you see in the field
A plant wilts in the day and does not recover overnight. Often one stem, or one side, droops first while the rest looks healthy. Then the whole plant dies. Leaves stay green or dull yellow as they wilt, usually around the time tubers form.

Cut a wilted stem across, near the soil. The ring of tissue inside is browned. Squeeze the cut end and a dull white, slimy mass oozes out. TNAU records the disease as favoured by 25 to 35 degrees C, humidity above 50 percent, and soil pH around 6.2 to 6.6.

### The glass-of-water test
CIP calls this the vascular flow test.
1. Pick a plant that is wilting but not yet dead.
2. Cut a piece of stem 2 to 3 cm long from the base.
3. Fill a clean, clear glass with clean water.
4. Hang the stem upright in the water. An opened paper clip holds it well.
5. Stand still and watch for a few minutes.

If the bacterium is present, thin milky threads stream down from the cut end, like smoke sinking through water. If nothing streams, the wilt is probably something else: Fusarium, Verticillium, or root damage. The test is a strong pointer, not proof; only a laboratory confirms it.

### On tubers
Cut a tuber across: the vascular ring looks water-soaked, then browns. At the eyes and stolon end, beads of ooze collect and soil sticks to them. A rotting tuber smells foul.

### Why you cannot spray this away
The bacterium lives in soil, on buried debris, and in the roots of weeds and other hosts. It also travels inside seed tubers, and such a tuber can look perfectly sound. CIP calls this latent infection, and names infected seed as the main route of spread. Nothing sprayed on leaves reaches bacteria inside the water-conducting tissue. Once a field is infested you manage it, you do not cure it.

### What actually works, and costs nothing
- Do not cut or split seed tubers at planting. NHB states plainly that splitting spreads the disease to healthy tubers.
- Pull wilted plants the day you see them, with their tubers, and bury them deep, downhill and away from irrigation channels. Not on the bund.
- After harvest of an infected crop, remove haulms and rotten tubers, then bury deep or burn.
- Never save seed from a field that had wilt, or from plants next to a wilted one.
- Weed before planting, and pull volunteer potato plants in the following crop. The bacterium survives in both.
- Wash soil off tools, plough, bullock hooves and your footwear before entering a clean field.
- Irrigate from a well, not a canal that has passed through an infested field. Water carries the bacterium downhill.
- Hand-weed instead of hoeing after emergence, and control root-knot nematode. Both wound roots, and wounds let the bacterium in.

### Rotation: which crops, and for how long
After an infected crop, do not plant potato or any other host for at least two years. CIP's guidance:
- **Good.** Cereals and grasses -- CIP names wheat and maize -- drop soil inoculum fastest; it writes that "rotation with cereals and gramineous pastures rapidly decreases soil inoculum potential". Onion and garlic are safe. So are cabbage and cauliflower, which also help clear weeds and volunteers. Peas and beans are safe and add nitrogen. Gourds are safe.
- **Never.** Tomato, brinjal, chilli and tobacco are all hosts. In warm low-lying areas where race 1 occurs, CIP says never follow with ginger or groundnut either.

Two years is a minimum, not a cure. Survival depends on soil and weather, so longer is safer.
