## Summary
Most late blight outbreaks start from an infected seed tuber or a nearby heap of waste potatoes. You can cut that risk before you plant, and you can make your field a harder place for the disease to spread. None of this costs a spray. All of it is worth doing whether or not you spray later.

## Body

### Start with the seed
Infected seed tubers are the main way this disease travels from one crop to the next. ICAR-CPRI scientists state that "the infected potato tubers are the primary source of inoculums for causing initial infection of late blight." AHDB gives the scale: "A single seed tuber with blight symptoms in every 100 tubers can produce at least two primary infection sources per hectare."

Buy certified seed where you can. UC IPM's rule is short: "Plant certified seed tubers." Never save seed from a crop that had blight.

### Check your seed yourself
Take at least 100 tubers at random from the lot. Wash them. Look closely at the eyes, the lenticels, and anywhere the skin was damaged. Cut a few open. AHDB calls this a washup test and says it is "simple and highly effective."

One warning for cold-stored seed. Cold hides the symptoms. AHDB notes that storage at around 3 °C "is likely to prevent the expression of tuber blight symptoms but the blight pathogen can remain viable," and advises warming a sample to about 15 °C for two weeks before judging it.

### Plant deep, and keep the ridge covered
Spores washed off the leaves must travel down through soil to reach your tubers. Soil cover is the barrier. AHDB advises avoiding shallow planting, placing tubers in the middle of the ridge, and cultivating deeply enough to cover them well. Rain later erodes the ridge, and dry weather cracks it. Both open a path down. Earth up again when the ridge thins. Manitoba Agriculture states it simply: "Use proper hilling to reduce infection in tubers."

### Give the canopy air
A dense, wet canopy stays wet for hours. That is when the disease sporulates. Manitoba Agriculture advises to "increase spacing of plants to reduce canopy density." UC IPM adds that "air drainage to facilitate the drying of foliage each day is important." AHDB notes potatoes planted beside trees often become blight hotspots, because the sheltered spot stays humid and plants dry slowly.

### Drain the field
Waterlogged soil raises humidity in the canopy and keeps the soil wet around the tubers. Clear your drains before the season. Well-aerated fields are among the measures the CPRI authors list.

### Water at the right time, in the right way
If blight is already in the crop, overhead watering makes it much worse. AHDB warns that "applying overhead irrigation to blighted crops can increase the risk of tuber infection considerably, because inoculum on the haulm is washed into the soil and onto progeny tubers." Furrow or drip watering does not wet the leaves. If you must use a sprinkler, never run it in the evening or when blight is visible.

### Rotate, and do not go back too soon
The CPRI chapter reports a clear link: "Generally, infection starts early in fields which are not used for crop rotations." Where both mating types of the pathogen occur together, the sexual spore can survive in soil for years. AHDB cites Dutch work showing oospores viable up to 48 months in sandy soil and 34 months in clay. A short rotation after a blighted crop is the riskiest combination.

### Do not over-feed nitrogen
Extra nitrogen makes soft, dense foliage that holds water. The CPRI authors recommend "avoiding use of excess nitrogen and use of moderate nitrogen fertilization."

### Scout every day once the canopy closes
Walk the field early in the morning. Check leaf undersides, low spots, ground near trees, and any waste heap. Finding the first patch is worth more than any single spray.
