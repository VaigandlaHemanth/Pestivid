## Summary
Not every sick-looking potato plant has a disease. Frost, heat, waterlogging, a nutrient shortage or spray drift from a neighbour's field can all copy the look of blight, virus or rot. These causes have no germ, so a fungicide will not help. Check the pattern in the field before you decide.

## Body

### First ask: is it a disease at all?
Four clues point away from disease.

- It appeared suddenly, right after a cold night, hail, heavy rain, or a spray nearby.
- Every plant in the patch looks the same, and the patch has a straight edge, or follows a headland, a low spot or the wind.
- There is no sign of a germ: no white fluffy growth under the leaf, no soft smelly rot, no insect.
- It is not spreading plant to plant.

SRUC's note on herbicide damage puts it plainly: "There are many environmental, physiological and agronomic factors that can mimic herbicide damage."

### Nutrient shortages: which leaves are hit
TNAU's guide separates deficiencies by where they show first.

- **Nitrogen** (older leaves): "Light green leaf and plant color with the older leaves turning yellow, leaves that will eventually turn brown and die."
- **Potassium** (older leaves): "On the older leaves, the edges will look burned, a symptom known as scorch."
- **Magnesium** (older leaves): "Older leaves will be yellow in color with interveinal chlorosis (yellowing between the veins)."
- **Boron** (young growth): "Abnormal development of the growing points (meristematic tissue) with the apical growing points eventually becoming stunted and dying."

This matters because the UNECE guide warns that early blight symptoms "may be confused with tomato spotted wilt and magnesium deficiency."

Never guess a fertiliser rate from a photograph. Get a free soil test through the Soil Health Card scheme and take the result to your Krishi Vigyan Kendra.

### Frost
Potato has almost no frost tolerance. Research on freezing in potato leaves reports that leaves "get frost killed at -3 degrees C and are unable to frost harden," and describes "the frozen leaves becoming completely water soaked and having a darkened appearance," with "Slight blackish discolorations" appearing within hours. That is very close to how late blight is described. But frost strikes the exposed top of the canopy and the low, cold parts of the field on one night, and it makes no white growth under the leaf.

On tubers, the UNECE guide notes that "Frosted tissue exudes water, and the edges of affected areas are blackened. There is often a clear demarcation line between healthy and affected tissue."

### Heat, uneven water and waterlogging
Misshapen and glassy tubers "are generally caused by uneven growth conditions, especially a warm period followed by rainfall." Cracks are often physiological, "caused by rapid tuber expansion often triggered by an increase in soil moisture following a dry period." Virus cracks are "usually indistinguishable from physiological cracking," so a cracked tuber alone proves nothing. Waterlogging leads to rot at harvest: tubers "exude water with a sour milk or vinegary smell." That smell points to drainage, not to a spray.

### Hail
Hail tears leaves and bruises stems, and looks the same on every plant. The wounds matter afterwards: breaks in the skin "can lead to secondary infection by rot pathogens." Hail is insurable under PMFBY, so report it within 72 hours.

### Spray drift and carryover
SRUC describes three patterns. Glyphosate gives "stunting and yellowing of newly developing foliage." Sulfonylureas give "a paling of the new top growth and cracking of the tubers usually in star shaped pattern." Clopyralid and aminopyralid "produce hormonal type symptoms with twisting, cupping and elongation of leaves and stunting of plants giving them a fern like appearance."

To confirm drift, SRUC advises: "Look for a pattern of symptoms in the field starting in the headlands and signs of weed kill around fences or hedges." Then check what neighbours sprayed, the wind direction, and spray records.

When you send this to an expert, photograph the whole plant and the whole patch, not one leaf.
