## Summary
Late blight is driven almost entirely by weather. It needs cool temperatures and long spells of very high humidity. Because those two things can be measured, the disease can be forecast days before you see the first spot. India has its own forecasting models built by ICAR-CPRI, called JHULSACAST and INDO-BLIGHTCAST.

### Why late blight is forecastable at all
The pathogen only makes spores when the air is near saturation. Those spores only infect a leaf that is wet. So the disease cannot start without a run of humid hours at the right temperature. Count those hours and you can predict the outbreak. This is why blight warnings exist and, say, virus warnings do not.

### The conditions that matter
TNAU lists the favourable conditions for India as relative humidity above 90%, temperature 10 to 25 °C, and night temperature around 10 °C, together with cloudiness before rain and rainfall of at least 0.1 mm.

CPRI scientists describe the pattern more fully: cool weather at 12 to 15 °C with humidity above 90%, with heavy dew or rain, alternating with warm moist spells at 18 to 20 °C. Humidity above 90% is needed for spores to form. Above 80% is needed for lesions to keep expanding. Cloudy weather helps the disease.

Field work in the North Eastern Himalayan region found disease severity strongly tied to temperature and morning humidity, with 91.6% of the variation explained by those two. At disease onset there, average minimum and maximum temperatures were 20.3 °C and 30.7 °C, with morning humidity 96.7%.

### The Smith Period
The oldest published rule is the Smith Period, defined in Great Britain by L.P. Smith in 1956. The James Hutton Institute states it as "two consecutive days where the minimum temperature is not below 10°C and there are at least 11 hours each day with a relative humidity 90%."

Modern strains of the pathogen infect in drier and cooler conditions than that. So Britain replaced it in 2017 with the Hutton Criteria: two consecutive days with a minimum temperature of 10 °C and at least six hours each day with relative humidity of 90% or more. Reading this in India: the Smith Period will under-warn you. Treat six humid hours, not eleven, as the danger line.

### India's own models: JHULSACAST and INDO-BLIGHTCAST
JHULSACAST was built at ICAR-CPRI using hourly weather data and is calibrated separately for each region. For the western Uttar Pradesh plains the rule is a 7-day moving total of 50 hours or more at 85% humidity or above, together with a 5-day moving total of 100 hours or more in the congenial temperature band of 7.2 to 26.6 °C. Disease then appears in 7 to 10 days. The thresholds differ for Punjab, the Uttarakhand tarai and the West Bengal plains, which is the model's main limitation.

INDO-BLIGHTCAST is the newer, web-based, pan-India model. It does not need hourly data or local calibration. It accumulates physiological days (P-days) and mean night relative humidity over seven consecutive days. Late blight is predicted within about 15 days once those running totals pass 52.5 P-days and 525 RH units for seven consecutive days.

### What to do with a forecast
A forecast tells you the region is at risk. It does not tell you your field is infected. Use it as the signal to start scouting daily, check leaf undersides in the morning, and get your preventive plan ready. Contact your Krishi Vigyan Kendra or state agriculture department for the current advisory for your district.
