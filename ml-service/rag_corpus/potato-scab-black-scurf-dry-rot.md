## Summary
Common scab, black scurf and dry rot all arrive on the seed tuber or live in the soil. None of them can be sprayed away once the crop is growing. Scab and black scurf mostly spoil how the tuber looks; dry rot destroys it in store. Clean seed, gentle handling and rotation do most of the work.

## Body

### Common scab (*Streptomyces* species)
Look at the tuber skin. You see rough, corky, tan to dark brown patches. They may be only a roughening of the skin, or raised corky pustules, or pits 3 to 4 mm deep ringed by hard corky tissue. Sometimes corky rings circle the lenticels, the tiny skin pores. In a bad attack, roots and stolons show dark lesions too.

NHB is blunt about the cost: the disease does not cut yield, it disfigures tubers, lowers market value and raises peeling loss.

What helps, at no cost:
- Use scab-free seed. The organism survives a long time on seed and in soil.
- Keep the ridge moist for a few weeks during early tuber formation. NHB says high moisture then makes conditions unfavourable to the disease. This is the most useful free action against scab.
- Scab is worse in alkaline soil. TNAU notes it is severe in alkaline conditions and advises avoiding alkaline fertilisers such as calcium ammonium nitrate.
- Rotate with wheat, pea, oats, barley, soybean, sorghum or bajra.

### Black scurf and stem canker (*Rhizoctonia solani*)
Black scurf is what you see on the tuber: hard, irregular, dark brown to black lumps stuck to the skin. Rub one with your thumb; it does not come off, and it will not wash off. These are resting bodies of the fungus, not a rot. The flesh underneath is usually sound.

The real damage happens earlier, underground. The same fungus attacks sprouts and stem bases. Infected sprouts discolour, and badly infected ones never reach the surface, so you get gaps in the row. Stems that do emerge may be girdled by a canker at the base. Those plants roll their leaves upward with a pink or purple margin, and often form small green or reddish tubers in the leaf axils above ground. Roots and stolons rot, so the plant roots poorly and makes deformed tubers.

TNAU gives moderately cool, wet weather around 23 degrees C as favourable.

What helps:
- Plant clean seed. Reject lots with black lumps on the skin.
- Plant shallow so the sprout reaches the surface quickly.
- Rotate with maize, or grow dhaincha (*Sesbania*) as green manure. NHB names both.

### Dry rot (*Fusarium* species)
This is mainly a store disease, and it needs a wound to start. The skin turns brown, then darker, and wrinkles. The wrinkles sit in rough concentric circles. Later a hole may open at the centre with white or pinkish mould in it. Cut the tuber and you find cavities of dry, brownish tissue. It ends dry, hard and shrivelled.

The fungus cannot get through unbroken skin. Every bruise, spade cut and drop is a door.

What helps:
- Let the skin set fully before you lift the crop.
- Do not drop, tip or throw tubers. Handle them like eggs.
- Wash seed tubers and dry them in shade.
- Avoid splitting seed at planting where you can.
- Hand-sort the store every few weeks and remove rotting tubers.

### Do not use mercury dips
Older Indian extension pages still list mercuric chloride and organomercurial dips for these diseases. Mercury pesticides are banned in India. Ethyl mercury chloride and phenyl mercury acetate appear on the Directorate of Plant Protection's list of pesticides banned for manufacture, import and use. Methoxy ethyl mercuric chloride is on the separate RESTRICTED list, not the banned one -- restricted means permitted under conditions, so check the current register rather than assuming either way. ICAR-CPRI moved seed treatment off organomercurials long ago. Ignore that advice wherever you find it.

### On rates
Boric acid and some fungicides are used as potato seed treatments in India for these diseases. This document records no concentrations or dip times. Get them from the CIBRC register or your Krishi Vigyan Kendra.
